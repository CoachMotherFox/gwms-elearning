Grappling With My Self is a 36-session program for boys. Three nights a week for twelve weeks, they wrestle, they talk, and they find out who they are when it gets hard.

What GWMS is

A youth program built on grappling, the kind of wrestling where nobody gets hit. The mat is the place where a boy finds out how he acts under pressure. The rest of the night is about what to do with that.

Every session is built around one question. Something like What makes a place feel safe to you? or What do you do when someone pushes back? The boys work the question with their bodies on the mat, talk it through with a counselor afterward, and answer it in their own words on their phone before they leave.

You vs You, Together.

That is the whole idea. You learn who you are by bumping into other people. A training partner makes you feel something: frustration, fear, pride, the urge to quit. Those feelings are not a problem to shut down. They are the material. You feel them with a real person in front of you, then you turn inward and work out where they came from. You cannot do that alone, which is why GWMS runs as a group and not a solo class.

What GWMS is not

  • Not a fight club. No striking. No tournaments. No rankings. Every round ends the instant someone taps, and everyone goes light, always.
  • Not a lecture. Coaches do not show a move and have kids copy it. The boys play games with rules, and they figure out the solutions themselves.
  • Not therapy. The talking part of each night is run by a licensed clinician or a trained facilitator, and the program's clinical content is reviewed by a licensed therapist. Coaches are coaches. They are not counselors, and they do not pretend to be.

How a night runs

Ninety minutes, the same shape every time. Boys know what is coming, and that matters.

  1. 0:00Warm up. They walk in and play. A coach gives the night's question and the night's three games while they move.
  2. 0:05Three games. Ten minutes each: standing, guarded, pinned. Each game has one win and a few rules. Partners switch roles after every win so every boy plays both sides.
  3. 0:35Cool down. Water, wipe off, sit. The body settles before the talking starts.
  4. 0:40Lesson. Thirty-five minutes with the counselor on the night's question. What happened on the mat, and where that shows up in your life.
  5. 1:15Phone. Five minutes. Each boy scans a code, reads a short recap of the night's lesson, answers one check, and writes his own answer to the question. No name goes with it.
  6. 1:20Clean up. Chore wheel, teams of three. Then the door.

Sites that can give two hours run a longer version with a rolling block added. Same order, same question.

The 36 sessions

Twelve weeks in three stages. Each stage takes the boy one step further, from showing up to carrying something out the door. The weekly themes are the questions the boys actually get asked.

Weeks 1 to 4

The Descent

Who you are when nobody knows you yet, and what your mask is protecting.

Arrival, Mask, Crack, Reveal

Weeks 5 to 8

The Initiation

What happens when someone makes real contact, pushes back, or breaks something with you.

Contact, Friction, Rupture, Repair

Weeks 9 to 12

The Return

What is different in you now, who needs it, and what you leave behind.

Emergence, Contribution, Legacy, Celebration

The same games come back across the twelve weeks with a harder question each time. By the end, a boy can hold his own in a round, get up when he is down, and tell you in his own words what he does when it gets hard.

For parents

Your son will be on a mat with other boys his size, playing games with rules, under coaches who are watching the whole time. Here is what we want you to know.

  • The tap is the law. Tap your partner, tap the mat, or say stop. Any one of those ends the round that second. It is the first thing taught, on the first night, and the room agrees to it out loud.
  • Nobody gets hit. This is grappling. Holding, moving, getting up. No punches, no kicks, and no squeezing on the neck, ever.
  • Everybody goes light. The games are built so that winning takes control, not strength. Coaches stop anything that looks like force.
  • The talking is private. What your son says in the lesson block stays there. What he types on his phone goes to the program with no name attached, so nobody can tell which answers are his.
  • Three nights a week, twelve weeks. Showing up is most of it. A boy who finishes all 36 gets a completion certificate. One who comes but does not finish gets an attendance certificate.

For schools, courts, and partners

GWMS runs in three settings with the same 36-session curriculum. What changes is pacing and pairing, not the program.

  • High school enrichment. An elective or after-school program.
  • Community youth programs. Delivered with a local partner organization, with intake and outcome data kept for reporting.
  • Juvenile diversion. Court-referred boys. Attendance is documented every night by the program's own rating form, and completion means all 36 sessions.

The design rests on published research: traditional martial arts training built around restraint lowers aggression where stripped-down training raises it (Trulson, 1986; Nosanchuk and MacNeil, 1989); three months of martial arts beat regular gym class on self-regulation and classroom conduct in a randomized study of 207 children (Lakes and Hoyt, 2004); and the two things that separate juvenile programs that work from ones that do not, a therapeutic philosophy and quality of delivery, are both design choices (Lipsey, 2009). GWMS is built on both.
